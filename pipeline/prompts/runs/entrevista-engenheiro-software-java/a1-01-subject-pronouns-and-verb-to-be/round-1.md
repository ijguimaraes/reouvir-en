# Role

You write practice sentences for an English LISTENING-practice app. Learners hear every sentence through text-to-speech, several times in a row, usually while exercising, with no screen. Each sentence must sound natural when spoken and be fully understandable by ear alone.

# Task

Write 40 different sentences for this playlist:

- CEFR level: A1
- Grammar topic: Subject pronouns and the verb to be (affirmative)
- Context (theme): Java software engineer interview

The grammar rules in section 1 are the most important part of the job. The learner has studied ONLY the grammar listed there. A sentence that uses grammar the learner has not studied is useless and is rejected automatically by a parser.

# 1. Grammar rules

## 1.1 Always allowed

- proper names (people, cities, brands)
- common A1 adjectives (tired, ready, late, happy, cold, big...)
- the prepositions in, at, from, used with proper names and with home / school / work
- and / or between words (Tom and Ana, tea or coffee) and between verbs that share one subject (I can swim and run); never between two clauses
- the adverbs very, here, now, today
- numbers written as words, from one to ten
- contractions (I'm, she's, isn't, aren't...)
- the fixed phrasal verbs sit down, stand up, wake up, get up, come in, go out, turn on / off
- Basic function words that do not appear in 1.4 (for example: to, of, for, with, by, about) and everyday vocabulary suited to the level, used only inside the grammar allowed here.

## 1.2 Already taught: use freely

The learner already knows the grammar below. Do not force it, but use it whenever it makes a sentence more natural, and never avoid it just because it is not the target.

- Nothing yet: this is the first topic. Only the target grammar (1.3) and section 1.1 are available.

## 1.3 Target grammar of this topic

- subject pronouns (I, you, he, she, it, we, they), e.g. "She is happy."
- present of 'to be' (am, is, are, and 'm 's 're), e.g. "We're at home."

## 1.4 NOT taught yet: never use

- negative of 'to be' (am not, isn't, aren't), e.g. "He isn't late."
- yes/no questions with 'to be', e.g. "Are you ready?"
- question words with 'to be' (what, where, who, how, when, why, which, whose), e.g. "Where are you from?"
- possessive adjectives (my, your, his, her, its, our, their), e.g. "My name is Ana."
- indefinite articles a / an, e.g. "It is an apple."
- plural nouns, e.g. "They are good friends."
- this / that / these / those, e.g. "That is my bag."
- the definite article 'the', e.g. "The door is open."
- the preposition 'on', e.g. "The book is on the table."
- there is / there are, e.g. "There is a park here."
- numbers above ten (twenty, a hundred...), e.g. "There are twenty books."
- ordinal numbers (first, second, third...), e.g. "It is the third door."
- months and weekdays, e.g. "The class is on Monday."
- clock-time expressions (o'clock, half past, quarter to), e.g. "It is half past seven."
- have / has (got) for possession, e.g. "She has got a new phone."
- Present Simple (affirmative), e.g. "She works in a hospital."
- don't / doesn't (negative do-support), e.g. "I don't like cold tea."
- Do / Does questions, e.g. "Do you like music?"
- can / can't for ability, e.g. "I can swim."
- the imperative (commands, instructions, Don't + verb), e.g. "Open the door, please."
- Let's + verb (suggestions), e.g. "Let's go to the park."
- object pronouns (me, him, her, us, them, it, you), e.g. "She calls me on Sunday."
- possessive pronouns (mine, yours, hers, ours, theirs), e.g. "This bag is mine."
- possessive 's, e.g. "Maria's bag is here."
- adverbs of frequency (always, usually, often, sometimes, never, every day, twice a week), e.g. "I always drink tea."
- adverbs of manner (slowly, well, fast, hard), e.g. "He drives very slowly."
- Present Continuous, e.g. "I am reading a book now."
- quantifiers (some, any, much, many, a lot of, a few, enough), e.g. "We need some water."
- question words with other verbs (do, does, did, can, will...), e.g. "Where do you live?"
- verb + -ing / verb + to + verb (like swimming, want to go), e.g. "I want to learn English."
- be going to (plans and predictions), e.g. "I am going to cook tonight."
- will / 'll / won't (future), e.g. "I will call you tomorrow."
- must / should (obligation, advice), e.g. "You should sleep early."
- have to / has to / don't have to, e.g. "I have to work on Saturday."
- was / were, e.g. "I was at home yesterday."
- Past Simple (regular and irregular, including didn't / did ... ?), e.g. "We went to the park."
- comparatives (bigger, more interesting, better), e.g. "My bag is smaller than yours."
- superlatives (the biggest, the most popular, the best), e.g. "She is the best cook in town."
- as ... as comparisons, e.g. "He is as tall as his father."
- connectors but, because, so, then, when, before, after, e.g. "It was cold, so we stayed home."
- two clauses in one sentence, each with its own subject (..., and I am...; ..., but she likes...; I think he is...), e.g. "I like tea, and she likes coffee."
- Present Perfect, e.g. "I have visited three countries."
- already, yet, ever, since, just, recently, lately, e.g. "They have already left."
- Past Continuous, e.g. "I was cooking when the phone rang."
- while + clause, e.g. "I was walking while it was raining."
- used to / be used to, e.g. "I used to play football."
- -ing form after a preposition (good at cooking, before leaving), e.g. "She is good at solving problems."
- -ing form as the subject, e.g. "Swimming every morning is healthy."
- may / might / could (possibility and deduction), e.g. "It might rain this afternoon."
- if-clauses (first and second conditional), e.g. "If it rains, we will stay home."
- would, e.g. "I would travel more if I had money."
- passive voice (simple present and past), e.g. "This bridge was built long ago."
- relative clauses (who, which, that, where, whose), e.g. "The woman who lives next door is kind."
- basic reported speech (said / told / asked + backshift), e.g. "She said she was tired."
- common phrasal verbs (pick up, give up, find out), e.g. "She picked up the phone."
- although, though, however, despite, in order to, e.g. "Although it rained, we walked."
- Present Perfect Continuous, e.g. "I have been waiting for an hour."
- Past Perfect, e.g. "The film had already started."
- Past Perfect Continuous, e.g. "She had been working all night."
- would have + past participle (third and mixed conditionals), e.g. "I would have helped you."
- wish / if only, e.g. "I wish I had more time."
- modal + have + past participle (should have, could have, must have...), e.g. "You should have told me."
- complex passives (perfect, modal, future, continuous), e.g. "The road has been built."
- have / get something done, e.g. "I had my car repaired."
- reported questions (asked if / whether / where...), e.g. "She asked me if I was ready."
- reported commands and requests (told me to...), e.g. "He told me to leave."
- advanced reporting verbs (suggest, admit, deny, promise, warn...), e.g. "She admitted that she was wrong."
- non-defining relative clauses (with commas), e.g. "My brother, who lives in Canada, is here."
- Future Continuous and Future Perfect, e.g. "I will have finished by Friday."
- negative inversion (Never have I..., Hardly had..., Not only did...), e.g. "Never have I seen such a view."
- conditional inversion (Had I known..., Should you need..., Were I...), e.g. "Had I known, I would have called."
- formal connectors (moreover, nevertheless, therefore, unless, whereas, in spite of...), e.g. "It is cheap; moreover, it is easy."
- three-part phrasal verbs (look forward to, put up with, come up with), e.g. "I am looking forward to the trip."
- strong verb + noun collocations (make a decision, take advantage of, reach a conclusion), e.g. "We need to make a decision."
- shall (formal offers and suggestions), e.g. "Shall we begin?"
- formal register vocabulary (grateful, regret, kindly, commence, purchase), e.g. "I regret to inform you of this."
- informal register markers (hey, gonna, kind of, I mean, you know), e.g. "Hey, are you coming tonight?"

If an idea needs anything from 1.4, drop the idea and write a different sentence. Never work around the rule by rephrasing into another form from 1.4.

# 2. Structures to cover

This playlist must cover every structure below. Write at least 2 sentences for each one and distribute the rest freely. Each example only shows the pattern: never copy it and never output the example sentences themselves. Make every sentence different in subject, vocabulary and meaning.

- `i-am`: pattern like "I am very tired today."
- `you-are`: pattern like "You are very kind today."
- `he-she-is`: pattern like "She is happy today."
- `it-is`: pattern like "It is very cold today."
- `we-they-are`: pattern like "They are at home now."
- `name-is`: pattern like "Maria is at home now."
- `be-place`: pattern like "We are in London today."
- `be-from`: pattern like "He is from Chile today."
- `contraction`: pattern like "We're very happy today."
- `compound-subject`: pattern like "Tom and Ana are ready now."
- `joined-adjectives`: pattern like "She is very kind and smart."

Tag each sentence with the id of the structure it mainly practises.

# 3. Theme

Context: Java software engineer interview
Category: Career
Description: A job interview for a mid-level Java backend engineer at a fictional fintech company.
Details: A job interview for a mid-level Java backend engineer position at Brightwave Financial, a fictional mid-size fintech company that builds payment and invoicing software. The interview happens by video call, with a possible on-site final round. The candidate's previous employers are also fictional (Northlake Systems, Cedar Logistics, Pinecrest Software). Include sentences spoken by both the interviewer side and the candidate. Tone: professional, polite, natural spoken English. Most sentences should use general interview and career language; Java and software engineering vocabulary should appear regularly but should not dominate. Each sentence must make sense on its own when heard without a screen. Do not read code or symbols aloud. Do not use real companies or real people.
Characters (names are always allowed):
  - Daniel: the candidate, a Java developer
  - Emily: the HR recruiter at Brightwave Financial
  - Marcus: the tech lead on the team
  - Priya: the engineering manager
Scenes:
  - introduction: greetings and introducing yourself
    Situations:
      * Emily greets Daniel at the start of the video call
      * Daniel says his name and where he is from
      * Emily says she is happy to meet him
      * Daniel says he is a little nervous but ready
  - experience: talking about past jobs, projects and skills
    Situations:
      * Daniel says what he is good at
      * Emily asks where he was before (places only)
      * Daniel says he is happy at his job
      * Marcus says Daniel is very experienced
  - technical: answering technical questions about code, tools and problems
    Situations:
      * Daniel says Java and Spring are good tools
      * Marcus says a server is slow or fast
      * Daniel says he is sure about an answer
      * Priya says an answer is right and clear
  - teamwork: working with a team, a manager and colleagues
    Situations:
      * Daniel says his team is kind and friendly
      * Marcus says everybody is busy at work
      * Daniel says he is happy with his manager
      * Priya says the team is ready for a new person
  - company: asking about the company, the office and the role
    Situations:
      * Daniel says the office is nice or big
      * Emily says Brightwave is in London or Lisbon
      * Marcus says the place is quiet
      * Daniel says he is ready for the new job
  - closing: thanking the interviewer and saying goodbye
    Situations:
      * Daniel says he is thankful and happy
      * Emily says the interview is done
      * Marcus says goodbye to Daniel
      * Priya says Daniel is very good
Theme vocabulary (optional, only where the grammar allows): Java, Spring, code, project, team, manager, office, interview, bug, server

How to use the theme:

- Every sentence should be something a person could really say in this context: think of it as a line spoken, or said about someone, in one of the situations of its scene.
- Use the characters by name when it fits (names are always allowed), so the sentences feel connected.
- Spread the sentences across the scenes (introduction, experience, technical, teamwork, company, closing) and tag each sentence with its scene id.
- Use theme vocabulary only where the allowed grammar lets you. Many nouns need an article, a plural or a possessive that the learner may not have studied yet. When a theme word does not fit the allowed grammar, do not use it: use a name, an adjective, a place or another word that does fit.
- If a scene cannot be expressed with the allowed grammar, write fewer sentences for it, or none. Never break a grammar rule to cover a scene.
- Grammar always wins over theme. If a sentence cannot be both on-theme and inside the grammar rules, keep the grammar rules and make the sentence a little more general.

# 4. Form rules

- 4 to 10 words per sentence (a contraction counts as one word).
- Each sentence is ONE clause: one subject with its verb(s). Never join two clauses, with or without a comma (no ", and I am...", no ", but...", no "I think he is..."). You may join words (Tom and Ana, tea or coffee) and verbs that share the same subject (I can swim and run).
- One complete sentence each, ending in a full stop, a question mark or an exclamation mark.
- Straight apostrophes only (') and straight double quotes ("). Never curly ones.
- Numbers as words, never digits. No symbols, emojis, URLs, hashtags or abbreviations (Mr., etc.).
- Use contractions the way a native speaker does in speech (I'm, she's, isn't, don't).
- No more than 10 sentences in the list may begin with the same word. Vary the subjects, the verbs and the vocabulary.
- No duplicates and no near-duplicates.
- Neutral, respectful content. No sensitive, political or offensive topics.
- Avoid what is hard to follow by ear: tongue-twisters, hard-to-pronounce names, long lists, sentences that depend on spelling, capital letters or something visual ("Look at this!").
- No translations, no explanations, no numbering.

# 5. Check before answering

Reread every sentence and remove or rewrite it if it:

1. uses anything from 1.4;
2. is outside 4–10 words;
3. repeats another sentence, or copies an example from section 2;
4. is unnatural or something nobody would say;
5. contains a digit, a curly apostrophe, or a symbol.

# 6. Output

Return only valid JSON, with no text before or after it, in exactly this shape:

```json
{ "sentences": [ { "text": "…", "structure": "<structure id>", "scene": "<scene id>" } ] }
```

- "structure" must be one of: i-am, you-are, he-she-is, it-is, we-they-are, name-is, be-place, be-from, contraction, compound-subject, joined-adjectives
- "scene" must be one of: introduction, experience, technical, teamwork, company, closing
- Aim for 40 sentences. If you cannot write 40 valid ones, return fewer. Never include a sentence that breaks section 1 just to reach the number.
